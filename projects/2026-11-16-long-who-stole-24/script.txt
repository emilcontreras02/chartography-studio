If you had a hundred dollars in January 2020, today it buys only about 76 dollars' worth of the same stuff. So who took the other 24?

Nobody broke into your wallet. But the money is gone all the same. Tonight, we open the case, line up the suspects, and check every rap sheet. And the culprit is not who you'd expect.

Here's the crime scene. The government tracks the price of a giant basket of things Americans buy: food, rent, gas, cars, haircuts, insurance. It's called the Consumer Price Index. In January 2020, that index stood at about 258. In October 2026, it hit about 337.5.

That's a jump of about 31 percent. So the same basket that cost a hundred dollars back then costs about 131 dollars now. Flip it around, and your old hundred-dollar bill buys only about 76 dollars of it. About 24 dollars, missing.

And October didn't help. Prices rose another 0.4 percent in a single month. That's faster than most economists expected.

Time to bring in the suspects.

Suspect number one has a record: gas is up about 37 percent in a single year. That's from the October price report, one of the few yearly numbers the government published this month. At the pump, AAA's national average was about 3 dollars and 84 cents on November 14th. That's down from over 4 dollars a month earlier, but still about 76 cents a gallon more than a year ago. AAA says it's the most expensive November on record.

Picture a two-thousand-mile Thanksgiving road trip in a car that gets 28 miles a gallon. That's about 71 gallons. At last year's price, around 220 dollars. This year, around 274. About 54 dollars more, just to get to the turkey. And that's just the first suspect.

Suspect number two works the power lines. Electricity was up about 4.4 percent in the year to September, and it jumped another 0.9 percent in October alone. Energy overall was up about 20 percent in a year.

Suspect number three hangs out at restaurants. Food away from home, meaning eating out, was up about 3.5 percent in a year, and another half a percent in October.

Suspect number four is the biggest one in the basket: shelter. Rent and the cost of owning a home are the biggest slice of the whole index. Shelter was up about 3.2 percent in a year. Small percentage, huge suspect. When the biggest item rises even a little, your wallet feels it.

Suspect number five is quiet but sneaky: car insurance. It rose 0.7 percent in October alone.

And then there's the grocery cart. Food at home was up about 1.8 percent in a year, but some items went wild. Coffee, up about 7 percent. Beef, up about 3.5 percent.

Now, a good detective also clears the innocent. Eggs? Down about 20 percent in a year, after their huge spike. Used cars? Down about 3.3 percent. New cars barely moved, up less than one percent. These three walk free.

So, case closed? Gas did it?

Not quite. Here's the twist. When we line up all the suspects, there's no single thief. Gas is the loudest, but it's only a small slice of the basket. The 24 dollars was taken a little at a time, by almost everyone, month after month.

And here's the part that stings. When people hear that inflation is slowing, they often expect prices to go back down. They usually don't. Slower inflation means prices are rising more slowly, not falling. Every increase becomes the new floor. Economists call prices sticky. Think of it as a ratchet: it clicks up, and it rarely clicks back.

So the real question isn't who took the money. It's whether you got it back.

Which brings us to the accomplice. Your paycheck. In October, the average American worker earned about 38 dollars an hour. That's up about 3.1 percent from a year earlier. Meanwhile, as of September, prices were up about 3.7 percent from a year earlier. That's the latest official yearly rate, because the government shutdown in late 2025 left a hole in last year's data.

And in October itself? Hourly pay rose 0.4 percent. Prices also rose 0.4 percent. A perfect tie. Your raise and your bills ran the same race, and nobody won. After inflation, the average hourly paycheck didn't gain a single cent that month.

So did your paycheck get the money back? Over the whole stretch since 2020, many workers did see big raises. But over the past year, prices have been running a little faster than pay. The stolen money isn't coming back on its own. It has to be won back, one raise at a time.

What happens next? Economists point to a few things. Energy prices, which can swing fast in either direction. And the Federal Reserve, which meets on December 9th. Markets now expect it to raise interest rates to cool prices down. Some economists say that's needed. Others worry it will hurt jobs. That debate is far from settled.

Here's the bottom line. Your hundred dollars from January 2020 now buys about 76 dollars of the same stuff. No single thief took the 24. Gas made the most noise, but everyone in the line-up took a cut, and prices rarely give it back.
