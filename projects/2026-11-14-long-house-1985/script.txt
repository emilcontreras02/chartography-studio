In 1985, a new American home cost about three and a half years of the typical family's income. Today, it costs about four and a half.

So buying a house was easier back then. Case closed? Not so fast. We ran three tests, and in one of them, your parents actually had it harder. The answer is not what you'd expect.

Let's build a time machine. We take one family, the typical American household, right in the middle of the income ladder. We send them shopping for a brand new home, once in 1985 and once in 2026. Same family, same goal, three rounds: the sticker price, the down payment, and the monthly payment. Whoever has the easier time wins the round.

Quick ground rules. We use the median price of new homes sold, from the Census Bureau, and the median household income, in the dollars of each year. No inflation tricks. Just price against paycheck.

Round one, the sticker price. In 1985, the median new home sold for about 84,275 dollars. The median household earned 23,620 dollars a year. Divide one by the other, and the house cost about three and a half years of income.

Now jump to 2026. The median new home in the third quarter sold for 393,300 dollars. The latest median household income, for 2025, is 87,460 dollars. That house costs about four and a half years of income.

Paychecks grew about 3.7 times over. New-home prices grew about 4.7 times. So almost a full extra year of pay, gone. If the family could save every single dollar, they'd need almost a year longer today to buy the same kind of house outright. Round one goes to 1985.

To put 20 percent down on a new home in 1985, a typical family needed about eight and a half months of income. Today it takes almost eleven. Here's the math. Twenty percent of 84,275 dollars is 16,855 dollars. On 23,620 dollars a year, that's about eight and a half months of everything the household earned. Now take today's new home at 393,300 dollars. Twenty percent is 78,660 dollars. On 87,460 dollars a year, that's almost eleven months. Before food, before rent, before anything else. Round two goes to 1985.

Two rounds down. Looks like a blowout, right? Here's where the time machine breaks down.

In 1985, mortgage rates were about 13 percent, and the monthly payment on a new home ate about 38 percent of a typical family's income. Picture a 30-year fixed loan for the other 80 percent of the price. In January 1985, the average rate was 13.1 percent. That's about 751 dollars a month, just principal and interest, on 23,620 dollars a year. About 38 percent of every paycheck. Now 2026. This week's average rate is 7.11 percent. That's about 2,117 dollars a month, on 87,460 dollars a year. About 29 percent. Today's bill is the bigger number. But 1985's bill took the bigger bite. In this round, your parents lost.

So here's the twist. The thing that made 1985 hard wasn't the house. It was the money you borrowed to buy it.

And that shows up again over the whole loan. Over 30 years, the 1985 family would pay about 203,000 dollars in interest alone. That's about 2.4 times the price of the house. Today's family would pay about 447,000 dollars in interest. A bigger pile of money, but only about 1.1 times the price of their house.

But high rates have one weakness. They can come down. By the last week of 1985, the average rate had already slipped to about 11.1 percent. A family that bought in January could later refinance into a cheaper loan. A high price is different. You can't refinance the price of a house. Once you've paid it, it's paid.

So who really had it harder? This is where people disagree, and both sides have a point.

One side says 1985 was easier. The house cost less, the down payment came faster, and a young family could get in the door. Once they were in, falling rates did the rest.

The other side says 1985 was harder. Every single month, more than a third of the paycheck went to the bank, and nobody knew rates would fall. In January 1985, a 13 percent mortgage looked like it might last forever.

Here's what the numbers say. The 1985 buyer had a harder month. The 2026 buyer has a harder start. Getting into the house is the hard part now, and staying in it was the hard part then.

And right now, the story is moving. Mortgage rates just fell for a fourth straight week, to 7.11 percent, according to Freddie Mac. A year ago they were 6.24 percent. Still higher than last year, but still nowhere near 1985. If rates keep falling, round three tilts even further toward today's buyers. If prices keep climbing, rounds one and two get even worse.

One honest footnote. Our payment math is an illustration: 20 percent down, a 30-year fixed loan, and no taxes or insurance. Real bills are higher in both years. This isn't advice, just the numbers.

So, the final score. Two rounds to 1985, one round to 2026. But the round that hits you every single month went the other way.

So here's my question for you. Would you rather have a cheaper house with a 13 percent mortgage, or today's prices with today's rates? Tell me in the comments.
