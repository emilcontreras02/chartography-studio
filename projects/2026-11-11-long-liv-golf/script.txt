Tiger Woods reportedly turned down somewhere between 700 and 800 million dollars to join a golf league. Four years later, that league is being liquidated.

It's called LIV Golf. In about five years, it spent more than 5 billion dollars. And on November 5, 2026, its lawyers told a US bankruptcy court in New Jersey that the league will wind down and sell off what's left. The hearing took less than 10 minutes.

So how does a sports league burn through 5 billion dollars and still end up in court? The answer is not really about golf. It's about a checkbook with only one name on it. And stay to the end, because the biggest winner in this story never signed anything.

Let's open the ledger. Almost every check in this story came out of the same wallet: the Public Investment Fund, Saudi Arabia's giant sovereign wealth fund. Keep an eye on that wallet. It's the only one that matters.

The league's pitch was simple. Shorter tournaments of 54 holes, a team competition on top, and no cut. No cut means every player in the field goes home with a check. Great for players. Expensive for whoever writes the checks.

Here's why that's strange. A normal sports league has lots of wallets paying in. Fans buy tickets. TV networks buy the rights to show the games. Sponsors pay to put their logos everywhere. LIV did land TV deals, first with The CW and later with Fox. But we could not find any public numbers showing the money coming in was anywhere close to the money going out.

In its first year, a brand-new golf league paid out 255 million dollars in prize money. Almost all of it came from one place: a Saudi fund. That first season had just eight tournaments. That's about 32 million dollars per event, on average. Then the schedule grew to 14 events a year, and the prize fund reached 405 million dollars. Then, in late April 2026, the wallet closed. The fund pulled its money. By early September, LIV had filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy. A business with one customer isn't really a business. It's an allowance.

Now, there was one last rescue attempt. An investment firm called BC Partners offered 300 million dollars to rebuild the league as LIV 2.0. But there was a catch. The deal needed a critical mass of players to commit. About 30 players reportedly signed on. Talks with about 10 others got stuck on money. Meanwhile, some of the biggest names, Jon Rahm, Tyrrell Hatton and Sergio Garcia, announced plans to leave for Europe's DP World Tour.

Then BC Partners walked away. It said mutually acceptable terms could not be reached. And on November 5, the checkbook slammed shut. As one attorney put it in court, "We believe the wind-down path is the best path."

So who gets paid now? According to court documents, LIV has somewhere between 100 million and 500 million dollars in assets. And it owes somewhere between 500 million and 1 billion dollars. That gap means not everyone gets their money back.

And there's a line. At the very front stands the fund itself. It's listed as a secured creditor, so in a liquidation it gets paid first. The players and vendors who are owed money stand further back. Golf Channel also reports more than 15.5 million dollars in retention payments to executives, including more than 6 million dollars to the CEO.

Let's do the math on that ledger. More than 5 billion dollars went in. At most, about 500 million dollars of assets are left. That means for every dollar spent, somewhere between 2 and 10 cents remain. Even in the best case, about 90 cents of every dollar are simply gone.

And here's another way to feel that number. Spread over about five years, 5 billion dollars is roughly a billion dollars a year. That's close to 3 million dollars, every single day, for five years straight.

Somebody offered Tiger Woods up to 800 million dollars. He said no. Back in 2022, LIV's boss at the time, Greg Norman, called the offer "high nine digits." Reports later put it at 700 to 800 million dollars. Woods stayed with the PGA Tour. To be fair, the players who did sign got very rich. But four years later, the league that made Tiger that offer is in bankruptcy court. And at least one of its former stars paid to get back in. When Brooks Koepka returned to the PGA Tour, the deal came with a 5 million dollar donation to charity. The price of coming back. In this story, the man who said no won.

So what really sank LIV Golf? Not the players, and not the prize money on its own. It was the shape of the business. One wallet paying out. Not enough wallets paying back in. When that one wallet closed, there was nothing underneath to hold the league up.

What happens next? The creditors and LIV meet on November 20, and the next bankruptcy hearing is set for November 24. The liquidation plan still has to be approved by the court, so the final chapter is not written yet. And since then, the Financial Times has reported that BC Partners is trying again, this time to take control through the league's bankruptcy loan.

But the lesson is already clear. Big money can buy players, trophies and headlines. It cannot buy a business model.
